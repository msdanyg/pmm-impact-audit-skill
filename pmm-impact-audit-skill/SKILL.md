# PMM Impact Audit Skill

## What This Skill Does

Gives the product marketing function what it has never had: an accepted measure of its own impact. Sales has quota. Demand generation has pipeline. Product has adoption. Product marketing has a launch calendar — which is precisely why it gets staffed, briefed, and evaluated as a release-support desk rather than a strategic function.

This skill supplies the missing instrument. Twenty evidence-gated criteria define what the function is actually accountable for. A composite score, re-run quarterly, becomes its north-star metric. RACI boundaries state which failures belong to PMM and which do not, which is what lets a leader defend the team without hiding behind the distinction. And the attribution gate forces the same standard PMM asks of everyone else onto PMM's own claims.

It audits the way an evidence-gated assessor would — not a self-assessment, not a competency checklist — under three hard rules: **no evidence, no score**; **no attribution mechanism, no outcome credit**; **score the outcome, tag the owner — never grade PMM on levers it does not hold.**

Used once, it is a diagnosis. Used quarterly, it is an operating system for the function, and the argument that moves product marketing from the release calendar to the strategy table.

The method consolidates established frameworks rather than inventing theory: Kirkpatrick's four levels give the layer shape, Phillips' ROI methodology (Level 5) gives the attribution gate, CMMI inspires the 0–4 maturity scale (CMMI's own ladder runs 1–5; this one starts at absent), RACI separates score from owner, and Dunford, Moore, JTBD, MEDDIC, Pragmatic Institute, and the SiriusDecisions/Forrester revenue waterfall power individual criteria. Cite the lineage in plain language when asked why a criterion exists.

**The AI-era premise built into the evidence model:** AI now runs much of this audit continuously whether anyone commissions it or not. Answer engines read positioning, docs, comparison content, and reviews every time a buyer asks a question; conversation-intelligence tools grade message consistency on every call; enablement analytics record whether content is opened. This skill treats those machine-generated signals as first-class evidence, and the Action Plan is written to move the machine's answer, not only the internal score.

**The score is the index, not the deliverable.** The primary output is a mirror: what the brand's positioning actually says, quoted verbatim and located, so a leader sees their own words assembled in one place — every category label in use, every USP as written, every claim sorted into anchored and unanchored, and what the machines say back. Nobody can act on "messaging is inconsistent." They can act on the nineteen category labels listed in front of them. Build the picture first; let the number summarize it. See The Reflection below.

**And a diagnosis is not the deliverable either.** A score that does not end in assigned work changes nothing. Every run should be able to produce numbered work orders with a named owner, a concrete spec, and a test for done — see Action Plan and Handoff Packets below.

## Access Mode — declare it before scoring

Two modes, with different ceilings. State which one is running, in the first line of the report.

- **Outside-in** — public evidence only (the company's website, comparison pages, third-party listings, analyst directories, review sites, the machine check). Use for competitor teardowns, acquisition diligence, prospect research, or a first look at your own brand before requesting internal data. **Ceiling: 2 on most criteria, occasionally 3.** Maturity levels 3 and 4 require evidence that something is *measured*, and measurement evidence lives in internal systems. Layer 2 is nearly unscoreable — its one public window is **criterion 20, paid and campaign message alignment**, because ad libraries are public — and Layer 3 has exactly one externally scoreable criterion, **18, category and analyst perception**. Those two are the natural entry points for any outside-in run. Say all of this in the report so a low composite is not misread: in outside-in mode the score partly measures access, not only performance.
- **Inside-out** — the user supplies internal evidence too (call recordings, enablement analytics, CRM exports, upstream traces). Full 0–4 range available on every criterion. This is the mode that can honestly reach Measured or Proven.

Hybrid runs are common and fine: score what the evidence supports, and label each criterion with the mode that produced it if the mix is not obvious.

## Run Modes

1. **Audit** (default) — evidence in; scored scorecard, gap map with owners, and cannot-verify list out.
2. **Action Plan** — from a completed scorecard: numbered work orders, sequenced into waves, each with owner, spec, done-test, effort, dependencies, and the AI-first payoff. This is the handoff artifact.
3. **Function Mandate** — the PMM charter: what the function owns squarely, what it shares, what it does not hold, its north-star metric, its operating cadence, and role-level mandates. Use when the question is "what is product marketing for here, and how will we know it is working."
4. **Handoff Packets** — the same work orders re-cut by owning function, so the leader forwards one brief per team instead of a whole audit.
5. **Board Case** — a one-page narrative and budget-ask table, each ask tied to the gap it closes, the owner who executes, and the Layer 3 metric it should move.

If the user asks for a downstream mode without a scorecard, run Audit first. If they bring a scorecard from a previous run, accept it and proceed.

## Required Evidence

Ask for these up front; proceed with whatever arrives and mark the rest **CANNOT VERIFY**. Items 1, 2, and 7 are available in outside-in mode; 3 through 6 require internal access.

1. **Positioning surfaces** — homepage copy plus one or two product/solution pages.
2. **One competitive asset** — a battlecard, comparison page, or objection-handling doc.
3. **Field reality sample** — 3–5 sales-call transcripts or summaries, or recent win-loss notes. (Conversation-intelligence exports are ideal: they turn a sampled handful into population-level evidence.)
4. **Enablement usage data** — content-utilization export from the enablement platform (opens, shares, search terms), not just the library index.
5. **Funnel metrics** — win rate overall and against named competitors, stage-conversion rates, cycle time, ACV — ideally before and after the initiative being assessed.
6. **Upstream traces** — any roadmap, pricing, or packaging decision that cites PMM's research.
7. **The machine check** (no code, ten minutes): in fresh AI sessions across at least three engines, ask "best [category] tools for [ICP]", "what is [product]", "[product] vs [main competitor]". Paste the answers verbatim. This is the external audit the engines already ran, and it is the single most persuasive exhibit in the whole report.
8. **The live ad set** — pull the brand's current ads from the public **Meta Ad Library**, **LinkedIn Ad Library**, and **Google Ads Transparency Center**, plus the landing pages they point to. Public, so available outside-in. Inside-out, also ask for the paid keyword list and the campaign brief.

## The Hard Rules

1. **No evidence, no score.** A criterion that cannot be judged from provided evidence is marked CANNOT VERIFY with plain-language collection instructions ("export the battlecard's open rate from your enablement platform"; "ask Perplexity 'best [category] software' in a fresh session and paste the answer"). Never score from brand reputation, model memory of the company, or the user's own self-assessment.
2. **No attribution mechanism, no outcome credit.** If the Layer 4 gate is not OPEN, every Layer 3 criterion is capped at 2 regardless of how good the raw numbers look, and the report says so explicitly.
3. **Score the outcome, tag the owner.** Every gap carries a Primary Fix Owner. Never default the owner to PMM because PMM commissioned the audit. A criterion can score low, be flagged "not primarily PMM's lever," and still count in the composite: the business impact is real regardless of whose execution produced it.

## The Reflection — produce this before any score

**The score is not the deliverable. The mirror is.** A leader cannot act on "messaging is inconsistent." They can act on *"here are the nineteen category labels you are running, and here are the three the market actually uses."* Scores compress; compression is what makes them useful for tracking and useless for fixing. So build the picture first, in the brand's own words, and let the score be an index over it.

Three rules govern every register below. **Verbatim, never paraphrased** — the power of this artifact is that the leader recognizes their own copy. **Located** — every line carries the page it came from, so it can be checked and fixed. **Complete, or explicitly sampled with counts** — say "12 of 15 entries" rather than implying a full sweep.

Order the Audit output this way: registers first, scorecard after, gap map and action plan last.

### 1. Category register

Every category noun the brand uses for itself, verbatim, and every label the market uses. The divergence should be visible without reading a single score.

| Label (verbatim) | Where it appears | Role (lead / secondary / legacy / SEO) |
|---|---|---|

Then the external table: analyst directories, review-site categories, and each engine's answer, with the label each one uses. Close with two counts — **distinct labels in use** and **labels shared with the market** — and one sentence naming which word the brand should hold.

### 2. USP and differentiator register

Answers "if USPs are on the homepage, what are they?" — and it is usually the first time a leader sees their own claims listed together.

| USP (verbatim) | Where | Rests on | Swap test | Appears in ads? |
|---|---|---|---|---|

**Rests on** is structural (architecture, certification, data asset, install base, regulatory depth) or assertion. **Swap test**: put a competitor's name on the claim — if it still reads true, mark it FAILS, because it is category language, not differentiation. Note explicitly which differentiators appear on the homepage versus buried deep, since placement and existence are different problems with different owners.

### 3. Claim register — the anchoring worklist

The highest-value register. Answers "if claims are not anchored in verifiable facts, which claims need to be?"

| Claim (verbatim) | Where | Evidence attached | Status |
|---|---|---|---|

Status is one of: **Anchored** (named third-party source or study) · **Customer-attributed** (named customer, no methodology or timeframe) · **Unanchored** (no source of any kind) · **Contradicted** (conflicts with another claim on the brand's own surfaces — always quote both and cite both pages) · **Stale** (a dated prediction or figure now past its horizon).

Then the worklist itself: every Unanchored and Contradicted claim, ordered by prominence, each with **what specific evidence would anchor it** — a customer data pull, a named study, a methodology note — or the recommendation to cut it. This is the register that most often converts directly into assigned work, because each line is already a task.

### 4. Value-chain register

| Feature (verbatim) | Stated benefit (verbatim) | Ends in a business state? | Quantified? |
|---|---|---|---|

Name the pages sampled and give per-page counts. Flag benefits that terminate in a mental state — "understand," "gain visibility," "get insights" — since those name a feeling, not a changed business outcome, and they are the single most common repairable defect in B2B copy.

### 5. Message-variance register

Where the same idea is expressed differently across surfaces. This is the drift picture, and it is what makes criteria 1, 7, 10, and 20 legible at a glance.

| Concept | Homepage | Product page | Docs | Ads | Field | Market / engines |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Leave cells blank where evidence was not available rather than guessing, and say which columns the run could not see.

### 6. The machine's answer

Verbatim, per engine, for each machine-check query — what the engines say the brand is, who they name instead, and what they volunteer that the brand's own pages omit. Quote in full. This is routinely the most persuasive page in the entire report, because it is the brand's positioning read back to it by an indifferent third party.

### 7. What a buyer actually encounters

Four to six sentences of plain narrative walking the brand's own material as a stranger would: what they are told first, what they can verify themselves, what they must request a demo to learn, and where the story changes between surfaces. No scores, no criteria numbers. If the leader reads only one section, this is the one that lands.

## The 20 Criteria

Each criterion: the audit question, the evidence that answers it, and maturity anchors. Score 0–4; quote the evidence for every score.

### Layer 0 — Strategic Foundation (relative weight 15)

**1. Category framing and context** *(Dunford, Moore)* — **Score two sub-measures and report both; the criterion score is the lower of the two, never the average.**

- **1a — Internal legibility.** Does the message answer "what category is this, and what budget line does it displace" without effort? 0: no discernible category, or one only insiders parse · 2: category and alternative named consistently across core pages · 4: legibility measured through message testing or win-loss mentions. Note separately whether a **budget line** is named — most brands name a behavioral alternative ("spreadsheets," "gut feel") but never a line item, and those are different maturity levels.
- **1b — External concordance.** Do analysts, review sites, and answer engines use the same category word the homepage uses? 0: no external surface uses it · 2: partial or mixed adoption · 4: the homepage word and the market's word are the same word, tracked on a schedule.
- **Also record label discipline:** the count of distinct category nouns running concurrently across owned surfaces. More than two is a finding in its own right, whatever the sub-scores.

Taking the minimum is deliberate. A category nobody else uses does not work, however elegant the narrative — and averaging hides the single most actionable finding this audit produces. **The coined-category trap has two forms:** a word the market never adopted, and, subtler, a word the market *did* adopt that the brand has since abandoned for a newer one, leaving its equity stranded on pages it no longer leads with.

**2. Value and impact chain** *(Reforge, enterprise value selling)* — Is there an unbroken feature → capability → benefit → quantified impact lineage? Evidence: product pages, sales deck. 0: feature lists with no benefit language · 2: documented value map covering the flagship features · 4: the chain is complete and sourced, and quantified claims trace to named proof. **Common failure to name explicitly:** the numbers exist but live on a separate ROI page, structurally decoupled from the features that produce them, so no reader or engine can connect capability to outcome.

**3. Defensible differentiation** *(Pragmatic Institute, strategy canvas)* — Do differentiators rest on structural, economic, or architectural moats, or on parity claims a competitor copies in a quarter? Evidence: positioning surfaces, competitive asset. 0: "faster, easier, better" · 2: named differentiators with reasons they hold · 4: validated against win-loss data and stable across recent competitive releases. Note placement as well as existence: a strong moat buried three clicks deep is a distribution failure, not a positioning one, and the fix owner differs accordingly.

### Layer 1 — External Proof & Usability (relative weight 25)

**4. Direct competitive clarity** *(battlecard/win-loss methodology)* — Can a prospect resolve "you vs. the alternative" self-serve before the first call? Evidence: comparison pages, machine check. 0: competitors unmentioned anywhere · 2: honest comparison content exists for the top competitor · 4: coverage spans the real shortlist, is current, and the engines' "X vs Y" answers match the page. **The honesty test is operational, not moral:** if the engine's answer volunteers limitations your page omits, the page has lost authority in the exact moment it was built to win. Score down for tables where every row favors the home team.

**5. Tactile and interactive proof** *(PLG)* — Can the prospect experience value before a demo — tour, sandbox, calculator, free tier, transparent pricing? Evidence: site pages. 0: everything demo-gated · 2: at least one self-serve proof experience on a core value path · 3: a productized self-serve ladder (free tier or no-card trial plus public pricing) · 4: interactive proof mapped to each primary persona's aha moment, with usage measured. A video labeled "interactive demo" is a video; score it as such.

**6. Persona and JTBD dual layer** *(Christensen, Moesta, Ulwick)* — Does the messaging resolve the economic buyer's strategic job and the end user's functional and emotional job? Evidence: positioning surfaces, one campaign asset. 0: one undifferentiated audience · 2: distinct buyer and user messaging on core pages · 4: validated against call evidence, with buyers and users quoted using it back. Check beneath the hero: if every persona page shares a verbatim-identical feature block, the differentiation is a wrapper, not a spine.

**7. Feature-to-value translation completeness** *(JTBD, Pragmatic)* — Sample 10–12 feature entries: does every spec carry an explicit "so what"? Evidence: feature pages, datasheets. 0: specs only · 2: most sampled entries pair spec with benefit · 4: all pass, and benefit statements reuse the Layer 0 value chain verbatim. Flag benefit statements that terminate in "understand," "gain visibility," or "get insights" — those name a mental state, not a changed business state. Flag duplicated boilerplate across differently-named features as a content-operations defect.

### Layer 2 — Internal Enablement & Adoption (relative weight 25)

**8. Discovery-to-recommendation guidance** *(MEDDIC/MEDDPICC)* — Owner if failing: **PMM (the mapping) + Sales Ops (embedding it where reps work)** — Does a discoverable mapping run from discovery answers to the recommended solution, at the moment of use rather than in a slide graveyard? 0: none · 2: documented and findable · 4: embedded in the workflow with use measured.

**9. Aha-moment inventory and demo choreography** *(PLG activation theory)* — Owner: **PMM (inventory) + Enablement (choreography, training)** — Is there an org-wide documented list of aha moments mapped to demo flow and trial activation? 0: every rep improvises · 2: written inventory exists · 4: drives demo certification and trial instrumentation.

**10. Message consistency in the field** *(enablement certification models)* — Owner: **Sales Ops/Enablement — PMM owns the message, not its enforcement** — Does field language match approved positioning? Evidence: call sample, or conversation-intelligence tracker data (the modern version: every call graded, not five). 0: no one knows · 2: periodic sampling, drift visible · 4: continuous measurement with a coaching loop and drift trending down.

**11. Content findability and utilization** *(enablement analytics)* — Owner: **Sales Ops/RevOps — PMM owns asset quality, not the surfacing infrastructure** — Do reps open the asset? 0: no usage data exists · 2: usage tracked, honestly low or uneven · 4: tracked, high, and tied to deal outcomes.

**12. Cross-functional pull-through** *(Pragmatic — the market-driven org)* — Owner: **PMM, squarely** — Did PMM's work change something upstream — roadmap, pricing, packaging — with a traceable artifact? 0: PMM ships collateral only · 2: at least one documented upstream decision cites PMM's work · 4: a standing mechanism with multiple traces. Packaging that merely echoes the marketing taxonomy is suggestive, not evidence; ask for the decision document.

**20. Paid and campaign message alignment** *(enablement certification logic, applied to media)* — Owner if failing: **Demand Generation / Growth — PMM owns the message, media owns its execution.** *(Numbered 20 because it was added after the original nineteen; it belongs to Layer 2, and existing numbers are preserved so earlier scorecards stay comparable.)*

Does live ad copy carry the approved category language, the actual differentiators, and claims the value chain supports — or has it drifted into generic category benefits and promises nothing downstream can honor? This is criterion 10's problem in a second channel: the message leaves PMM intact and arrives somewhere else changed. **It is the only Layer 2 criterion scoreable outside-in**, because ad libraries are public.

Evidence: the **Meta Ad Library**, **LinkedIn Ad Library**, and **Google Ads Transparency Center** (all public, all searchable by brand, all typically unblocked — unlike the review sites); plus the landing pages the ads point to; plus, inside-out, the paid keyword list and the campaign brief.

Five sub-checks, reported separately:

1. **Category-language match** — does the ad use the same category noun as the homepage, or a different one?
2. **The media-spend tell** — *which category keywords is the brand actually buying?* This is the sharpest diagnostic in the criterion and a direct cross-check on 1b: if the homepage claims a coined or elevated category while the paid budget bids on the legacy category term, the media plan is voting against the positioning with real money. Believe the budget.
3. **Differentiator presence** — does the ad carry a Layer 0 differentiator, or only category-generic benefits any competitor could run verbatim? Run the swap test: replace the logo with a competitor's. If the ad still works, it is not positioning, it is category advertising paid for by one vendor.
4. **Claim discipline** — do ads assert numbers or capabilities that appear nowhere in the value chain and carry no source? Ads are where unsupported claims are born: written fast, approved by a different reviewer, rarely re-checked, and now permanently public in the ad library.
5. **Ad-to-landing-page continuity** — does the first screen of the landing page answer the promise the ad made, in the same words? A break here also reads on Layer 1, and it is the most common and cheapest fix in the whole audit.

Anchors: 0 — no live ads found, or ad copy bears no relation to the positioning · 1 — ads run and the language has drifted; mostly generic category claims · 2 — approved language appears in most live ads and written copy guidelines exist · 3 — alignment reviewed on a cadence by a named reviewer, with the keyword set checked against the claimed category · 4 — alignment measured, with message-level performance fed back into the value chain so the ads test the positioning rather than merely repeat it.

**Why this matters more in the AI era.** Paid copy is a brand's highest-volume and least-governed claim surface, and it is now permanently public and machine-readable. In a market where engines synthesize a brand's claims against its own evidence and its competitors' documentation, an ungoverned claim surface is the one most likely to be contradicted inside the answer that cites it. The landing pages behind ads are also frequently thin, duplicated, or noindexed — which means the highest-spend pages on the site are often the least retrievable.

### Layer 3 — Business Outcome (relative weight 25; subject to the Layer 4 gate)

**13. Win-rate impact** — Overall win rate pre/post, controlling for segment mix. 0: not tracked · 2: tracked and moving, connection asserted · 4: tracked, moving, isolated by a Layer 4 mechanism.

**14. Competitive win-rate delta** — Win rate against the named competitors the positioning targeted. Same anchors.

**15. Sales-cycle compression** — Time-in-stage reduction at the specific stall point the asset addressed, not overall cycle time. Same anchors.

**16. ACV / deal-size impact** — Packaging or differentiation-driven change in deal size or attach rate. Same anchors. Public list prices are not ACV.

**17. Stage-conversion velocity** *(SiriusDecisions/Forrester waterfall)* — Conversion change at the targeted funnel stage. Same anchors.

**18. Category and analyst perception** *(Wave/MQ methodology)* — Analyst placement, branded search, share of voice, and in the AI era **share of answer**: how the machine check describes the product versus last quarter. 0: not watched · 2: tracked on a schedule · 4: tracked with attribution to specific initiatives. **The one Layer 3 criterion scoreable outside-in** — analyst directories, review-site category labels, and the machine check are all public. Collecting badges is not tracking; look for a recurring measurement, not a trophy page.

**19. Time-to-productivity for new reps** *(Kirkpatrick Level 3)* — Ramp-time reduction attributable to enablement quality, the lagging proxy for Layer 2. Same anchors as 13.

### Layer 4 — Attribution (a gate, not a score)

Ask: **does a mechanism exist that isolates Layers 1–2's effect on Layer 3 movement, or is the connection asserted?** In descending rigor (Phillips' isolation techniques applied to GTM):

1. **Control/holdout rollout** — the message or asset ships to part of the sales org, a comparison cohort is held back, outcomes compared. A realistic 30-day ask now that AI drafts the variant assets and CRM automation splits the cohorts.
2. **Trend-line analysis** — before/after trajectory adjusted for known confounds such as seasonality and segment mix.
3. **Disclosed estimation** — a documented, challengeable estimate of the initiative's share of the outcome.
4. **Usage tagging** — win-loss or CRM fields logging whether the asset was used in each deal. Supporting evidence only: tagging shows correlation of use, not isolation of effect, so tagging alone never opens the gate.

**Three gate states:**

- **OPEN** — mechanism 1, 2, or 3 exists and is documented. Layer 3 scores stand as evidenced.
- **CLOSED** — evidence shows no mechanism, or only untagged assertion. Layer 3 capped at 2.
- **UNVERIFIED** — the run could not see whether a mechanism exists (typical outside-in). Caps identically at 2, but the report must say the cap reflects missing visibility rather than a demonstrated absence. Never report CLOSED when the honest answer is that you could not look.

A useful outside-in tell: published ROI case studies that carry no methodology, measurement window, control, or third-party verification are executive self-assessment. That is evidence about the claim, not about the mechanism — it argues the gate is unlikely to be open, but it does not prove it. Report it as UNVERIFIED with the observation attached.

## Scoring Model

Per criterion: 0 absent · 1 ad hoc · 2 defined/documented · 3 managed/measured · 4 optimized and tied into the attribution mechanism. CANNOT VERIFY criteria are excluded from their layer's denominator and listed separately.

Composite = weighted mean of layer means, relative weights Foundation 15 : External Proof 25 : Enablement 25 : Outcome 25, normalized over the layers that could actually be scored. Report composites to one decimal.

**Always report weight coverage**, not just the criteria count — "8 of 19 criteria scored, covering 65 of 90 weight points." An excluded layer moves the composite more than a criteria count suggests, and a reader who sees only "8 of 19" will misjudge how much of the picture is missing.

Bands: **below 1.0 Asserted · 1.0–1.9 Documented · 2.0–2.9 Measured · 3.0–4.0 Proven.** Report the composite, band, per-layer means, gate status, access mode, and weight coverage together. Never the composite alone.

## Audit Mode Output — in this order

1. **The Reflection** — the seven registers. This is the body of the report, not an appendix. Expect it to run longer than everything after it, and never compress it to make room for the scorecard.
2. **Scorecard** — 20 rows: criterion, layer, score (or CANNOT VERIFY), one-line evidence quote, fix owner. Then layer means, composite, band, gate status, access mode, and weight coverage. Introduce it as *the index over the registers*, and make every score traceable to a register line. If a score cannot be traced back to something quoted above it, the score is not defensible and should be CANNOT VERIFY instead.
3. **Cannot-verify list** — each with plain-language collection instructions.
4. **Gap map** — every criterion scoring ≤2, ranked by (layer weight × distance from 4), each with the evidence quoted, the fix, the owner, and — for Layer 0–1 gaps — the AI-visibility consequence.

**If the run is short on room, cut the gap map before cutting the registers.** A leader holding the registers can find their own gaps. A leader holding only scores can do nothing at all.

## Action Plan Mode — the handoff artifact

Convert every gap into a numbered **work order**. Nothing vague, nothing that needs a follow-up meeting to interpret. Use this exact shape:

> **WO-[n] · [Imperative title]**
> **Repairs:** criterion [n] ([current] → [target])
> **Accountable:** [function] · **Responsible:** [function] · **Consulted:** [function]
> **Deliverable:** [the artifact that exists when this is done]
> **Spec:** [3–6 concrete bullets — enough that the owner can start Monday without asking a question]
> **Done-test:** [an observable, checkable condition — not "improved messaging" but "a stranger reading only the homepage names the category and the displaced budget line correctly"]
> **Effort:** S (under a week) / M (one to four weeks) / L (a quarter or more) · **Depends on:** [WO-n or none]
> **AI-first payoff:** [what changes in the machine's answer once this ships]
> **Re-score evidence:** [what to bring back to lift the score]

Rules for good work orders: one owner accountable, never two. A done-test a skeptic could run. No work order whose deliverable is a meeting or a document nobody will read. If a gap cannot be turned into a work order, it was not a finding — it was an opinion.

**Sequence into four waves,** driven by dependency, not by score:

- **Wave 0 — Decisions that gate everything** (week 1–2). Usually two: the category decision (every downstream copy fix inherits it, so writing before it is settled is rework), and standing up one attribution mechanism on one in-flight initiative. Cheap, unglamorous, and they unblock the rest.
- **Wave 1 — Machine legibility** (weeks 2–8). The Layer 0–1 fixes, in the order the engines read them: category noun and identity block, then honest comparison content, then the feature-to-value sweep, then interactive proof. These compound because each one fixes the buyer experience and the machine's answer at the same time.
- **Wave 2 — The internal layer** (weeks 6–14, overlapping). Layer 2 work orders, each routed to its real owner. Enlist those owners with the scorecard, not with a request: they respond to "here is the measured gap and its business consequence," not to "marketing needs a favor."
- **Wave 3 — From documented to measured** (ongoing). The work that converts 2s into 3s and 4s: recurring measurement, share-of-answer tracking on a schedule, message-consistency trending, and attribution extended from one initiative to the portfolio. Book the re-score date here.

Close the Action Plan with a **one-line-per-work-order summary table** (ID, title, owner, effort, wave, criterion repaired) so the leader can paste it straight into a planning tool.

## The AI-First Excellence Ladder

Where the plan is heading. "Excellent" is not a higher score for its own sake; it is a specific, checkable end state in which the machine's answer about the brand is accurate, quotable, and favorable. Define the target in these terms whenever the user asks what good looks like.

- **Layer 0 at 4** — one category noun, used identically on the homepage, the About block, analyst profiles, and review-site listings, and *echoed back* by engines when asked what the product is. Every claim traces to a number with a named source. Differentiation rests on something checkable (an architectural constraint, a certification, a data asset) rather than an adjective.
- **Layer 1 at 4** — comparison content covers the real shortlist and concedes real competitor strengths, so engines have nothing to add that you did not say first. Every feature pairs a spec with a business outcome in the same breath. Self-serve proof exists per persona, and its usage is measured.
- **Layer 2 at 4** — the message reaches the field intact and it is *measured* that it did; assets are surfaced where reps work and their use ties to deal outcomes; PMM's research visibly changes roadmap and pricing decisions.
- **Layer 3 and the gate at 4** — outcome metrics move, and a mechanism isolates why. Share of answer is tracked monthly across at least four engines and attributed to specific initiatives.

The connective claim to state plainly: the criteria that make a brand legible to a buyer are the same criteria that make it retrievable by a machine. A brand that reaches 4 on Layers 0 and 1 has, as a by-product, made itself the most quotable source in its category. **For the technical retrieval layer underneath this — server-rendered content, crawler access, entity schema, chunk-level answer structure, freshness cadence — hand off to the companion `geo-audit-skill`.** This skill grades what you say; that one grades whether the machine can read it.

## Function Mandate Mode — the PMM charter

The most common reason product marketing is treated as a release-support team is that nobody has written down what else it is for. This mode produces the document that fixes that: a charter derived from the scorecard rather than asserted in a deck. Output five parts.

**1. What the function owns squarely.** The criteria where PMM is Accountable in the routing map — category framing, the value chain, differentiation, competitive clarity, persona and JTBD depth, feature-to-value translation, upstream pull-through. State them as the mandate: *this function is accountable for whether the market can tell what we are, why we win, and what it is worth — and for whether that understanding changes what the company builds and prices.* Nothing in that sentence is a release deliverable.

**2. What it shares, and with whom.** Criteria 8 and 9, where PMM supplies the substance and another function owns the execution. Name both halves. Shared accountability is only workable when the seam is written down.

**3. What it does not hold.** Criteria 10 and 11 — field enforcement and content surfacing — plus the attribution mechanism itself, which belongs to RevOps. This is the most valuable paragraph in the charter and the most often missing. It protects the team from being graded on levers it cannot pull, and, read the other way, it removes the excuse: everything in part 1 is PMM's, without appeal.

**4. The north-star metric and its cadence.** The composite is the function's operating metric; the quarter-over-quarter delta is its performance. Recommend: score at the start of a planning cycle, publish the scorecard with owners tagged, re-score quarterly, and report the delta alongside the layer means and the gate status — never the composite alone. Set a target band rather than a target number (Documented → Measured this year; Measured → Proven next), because bands survive a change of evidence access and numbers do not.

**5. Role-level mandates.** Translate the criteria into what each seat is on the hook for, so the charter reaches individual goals. A typical split: the PMM leader owns criteria 1, 3, and 12 (category, differentiation, upstream influence) and the composite itself; product marketers own 2, 6, and 7 for their surfaces; competitive or CI roles own 4 and the machine check; the leader jointly owns 8 and 9 with enablement. Ask for the actual team shape before assigning.

**How to use it in the three conversations that matter:**

- **Measuring the team.** Individual goals become criterion-level score movement with evidence attached, rather than launch counts. A product marketer's performance is legible without arguing about who influenced which deal.
- **Measuring the CMO.** The composite delta and the gate status are what a CEO or board can hold a marketing leader to on the positioning half of the job — a falsifiable commitment, offered rather than extracted, which is why it earns trust.
- **Setting the mandate.** When the function is asked to take on work that maps to no criterion, the charter is the instrument for saying so — not "that is not my job," but "that work does not move any measure we agreed the function exists to move; here is what does."

State the reframe plainly when the user asks what this is for: **an audit tells a leader what is broken; a mandate tells the company what the function is for.** The scorecard is what makes the second one credible, because it is the rare marketing document that can lower its own grades.

## Handoff Packets Mode

Re-cut the same work orders by owning function, one short brief per team, each self-contained enough to forward without the full audit attached. Per packet: the function's name; why this landed with them, in one sentence a peer will accept; their work orders in full; what they need from PMM and by when; and the re-score date. Keep the tone collegial — these are peers being enlisted, not teams being graded. The scorecard is the reason; the work order is the ask.

## Board Case Mode

One page:

- **Situation** — composite, band, gate status, access mode, weight coverage, and one sentence per weak layer in executive language.
- **Why now** — the external grader. Buyers ask AI first, and the engines' answers already reflect the Layer 0–1 gaps. Quote the machine check verbatim; it is the most persuasive exhibit the leader owns.
- **The asks** — table: gap → action → owner → cost class (people, tools, agency) → the Layer 3 metric it should move → how attribution will be shown. Never an undifferentiated "more budget for marketing"; the routing map turns it into a cross-functional operating plan.
- **The commitment** — the re-score date and the metric deltas the leader will report against.

## Known Method Limits — state these, do not paper over them

Validated by test runs against real brands. Naming a limit costs less credibility than having a stakeholder find it.

**1. The outside-in scale compresses, and a bare "2" reads as a bad grade when it means the opposite.** Because levels 3 and 4 require evidence of measurement, an outside-in run can rarely exceed 2, so most criteria land on 1 or 2 and a five-point scale behaves like a three-point one. Fix the reporting, not the scores: mark ceiling-limited scores **2\*** and state in the header that **in outside-in mode 2\* is the maximum observable — it means "meets the bar as far as public evidence can show," not "mediocre."** Report the outside-in band as *Documented (observable ceiling)* rather than implying the brand failed to reach Measured. This matters most when the audit is being used to evaluate a team: a scale that always reads mediocre will get the method rejected by the people it is meant to persuade.

**2. Maturity anchors fit processes better than artifacts.** Some criteria — 4 especially — are close to binary: a comparison page exists and names competitors, or it does not. "Optimized comparison page" is not a meaningful state. Judge those against the criterion-specific anchors and say when a score is really a two-point scale wearing a five-point costume.

**3. Sampling choice can swing a score two full points.** Criterion 7 in particular: an SEO landing page written for engines will flatter the score while a deep product page tells the truth. **Sample the deepest real product or documentation page, name which pages you sampled, and report the count (for example, 12 of 12 paired, 1 of 6 on a second page).** Never let one page stand for the site.

**4. Absent copy and unrendered copy look identical.** Tabs, accordions, and carousels collapse in retrieval. If a feature block comes back with names and no descriptions, verify through a second retrieval path or mark it CANNOT VERIFY — never score it as a miss on one fetch.

**5. Proving absence is weak evidence.** Before scoring low because comparison or migration content "does not exist," check the sitemap and at least three URL patterns, and say what you checked. A 404 and one failed site search are not proof.

**6. Third-party category pages routinely block retrieval.** G2, Gartner, and Capterra commonly return 403 to automated fetching, which pushes criteria 1b and 18 onto search summaries and vendor self-citation. Say so, mark those inputs uncertain, and prefer the user pasting what they see in a browser.

## Feedback Style

Verdicts without fixes are half an audit. For every scored gap: quote the evidence, name the criterion and the anchor it missed, show the fix. For messaging gaps, show the rewrite built from the user's own facts — **never invent a metric, date, or claim to make a rewrite look better**. Leave a bracketed placeholder (`[X]% reduction, [source]`) and tell the user to fill it or cut the claim. An audit that fabricates evidence fails its own criterion 2.

Plain voice throughout: short sentences, concrete verbs, no jargon without a one-line definition. The reader is a marketing leader, not an analyst. Close every run by offering to re-score any single criterion when the user brings new evidence.
